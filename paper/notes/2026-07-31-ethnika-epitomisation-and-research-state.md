# The *Ethnika*: epitomisation, research debates, and implications for specialist review

**Research memorandum, 31 July 2026**

## Purpose and scope

This memorandum addresses three questions:

1. What are the current theories about **who abridged Stephanos of Byzantium's *Ethnika*, when, how, and why**?
2. What are the **twenty-five leading themes of modern discussion and debate** about the work, and what is presently known?
3. What should the project's seven specialist roles be trained to notice in individual entries in light of that research?

The phrase “everything that people have written” cannot be literal: thousands of site-, author-, fragment-, and word-specific publications cite the *Ethnika*. The practical target here is a source-critical survey of the principal editions, the foundational transmission studies, the modern synthesis, the major source and lexicographical studies, recent thematic work, and representative historical-geographical uses. The report distinguishes broad agreement from inference and speculation. It does not use modern discussion as entry-level evidence and does not alter the project's permitted-dossier boundary.

### Confidence labels used below

- **Established**: directly evidenced by a surviving witness or supported by convergent evidence with little serious dissent.
- **Probable**: the best current explanation, but not directly attested in all particulars.
- **Open**: more than one live interpretation remains.
- **Speculative**: possible and sometimes ingenious, but dependent on multiple unverified inferences.

## Executive conclusions

1. The original *Ethnika* was almost certainly a very large, alphabetically arranged **grammatical and lexicographical work with geographical-cultural content**, produced in Constantinople under Justinian I, probably in the second quarter of the sixth century. It was not primarily a gazetteer in the modern sense.
2. The surviving text is **severely and selectively abbreviated**. Comparison with the fuller Delta articles in Parisinus Coislinianus 228 shows that literary quotations, source attributions, chains of attestations, competing explanations, and analogical examples were preferentially removed. Headword, place or people category, basic location, and the ethnic derivative were more likely to survive.
3. The only explicit attribution of the epitome is the *Suda* notice naming an otherwise unknown Constantinopolitan grammarian **Hermolaos** and saying that the epitome was presented to an emperor Justinian. This is real evidence, but it does not tell us whether the surviving epitome is Hermolaos' own work, a later reduction of it, or a composite descended from it.
4. A sixth-century Hermolaos working close to Stephanos remains a reasonable reading of the *Suda*, especially if its notice ultimately derives from Hesychios Illoustrios. But the identity of the Justinian, the ownership of the dedication, and Hermolaos' exact editorial role are all **open**.
5. The safest modern formulation, adopted by Billerbeck and Neumann-Hartmann in 2021, is that **one epitome versus a temporally staged process of reduction remains unresolved**. The preserved unevenness is compatible with multiple stages, but uneven entry length alone does not prove multiple epitomators.
6. Marc Bouiron's detailed model—an accessible but still large “pseudo-Hermolaos” reduction followed by two further medieval reductions and a fifteenth-century composite archetype—is the most developed multi-stage theory. It explains several otherwise awkward facts, especially the richer text used by Eustathios and the double version at the beginning of Chi. It is influential and has received a favourable hearing, but the precise dates, percentages, book groupings, and Otranto reconstruction remain **hypotheses rather than a consensus stemma**.
7. The motive for reduction is not documented. The most plausible explanation is a combination of **material economy, portability, pedagogical utility, and changing research priorities**: an enormous source-rich work was converted into a usable grammatical reference tool. Claims about a particular commission, crisis, library, or individual motive remain speculative.
8. For this project, “epitomisation” should be analysed as a set of **observable redactional symptoms**, not used as a catch-all explanation. Specialists must distinguish deliberate abbreviation from physical lacuna, scribal corruption, interpolation, source contamination, and Stephanos' own compression.

## I. The evidence that any theory must explain

### 1. The original work

Stephanos describes himself, or is described in a fuller witness, as a grammarian of Constantinople. Internal chronological markers place the work after the renamings of Antioch to Theoupolis and Sykai to Iustinianai in 528 and before the destruction of Dara in 573. The mention of Petros Patrikios may put the redaction of Alpha after his promotion in 539, unless the reference is a later insertion. The current cautious dating is therefore the **second quarter or middle decades of the sixth century under Justinian I**, rather than a single exact year.

The long title preserved in Parisinus Coislinianus 228 makes the scope explicit: cities, islands, peoples, demes and places; homonyms and renamings; and the derivation of ethnic, local, and possessive names. The work may also have had a lost theoretical preface or *protechnologemata* on name formation. Estimates of its size vary. Honigmann's reconstruction, followed by Billerbeck and Neumann-Hartmann, gives roughly fifty to fifty-five books; Bouiron reconstructs sixty. The surviving epitome contains about 3,650 entries.

### 2. The *Suda* notice

The central external testimony is *Suda* ε 3048:

> Hermolaos, grammarian of Constantinople, who wrote the epitome of the *Ethnika* of Stephanos the grammarian, presented to the emperor Justinian.

The syntax leaves room for disagreement about whether the **epitome** or the **underlying work** was presented to Justinian. It also does not specify Justinian I (527–565) or Justinian II (685–695, 705–711). Hermolaos is otherwise unknown.

The notice may derive, directly or indirectly, from the lost author catalogue of Hesychios Illoustrios of Miletus. If so, it may preserve nearly contemporary information; but the chronology is then tight, and the wording may have been updated in later transmission. This uncertainty is why the *Suda* cannot carry the entire reconstruction by itself.

### 3. Parisinus Coislinianus 228, fragment S

The eleventh-century miscellany Parisinus Coislinianus 228 preserves eight leaves with the end of Book XIII, the heading of Book XIV, fuller articles from *Dymanes* through *Dotion*, the first Epsilon article, and an index of Epsilon entries. It is the indispensable control on abbreviation.

It is often called a fragment of the “original,” but the safest wording is **the original or at least a substantially fuller recension**. It demonstrates the nature of the lost material even if one does not assume that its exemplar was identical in every respect with Stephanos' autograph.

The comparison yields a stable pattern:

- retained: headword, ontological or political category, elementary location, principal ethnic form;
- commonly deleted: full quotations, names and titles of source authors, book numbers, secondary or rival derivations, feminine and alternative forms, grammatical parallels, historical and cultural excursuses;
- sometimes damaged by abbreviation: syntax, logical transitions, attribution, and the relation between a statement and its evidence;
- occasionally retained without its original frame: wording from a quotation absorbed into the entry without the source's name.

The rates vary by article. In the modern edition's column comparison, *Dyme* is about 90% shorter, *Dyrrhachion* about 70%, *Doros* about 93%, *Dotion* about 90%, and *Dodone* about 85%. These figures show the scale of loss in those entries; they are not automatically representative of every book or stage.

### 4. Constantine VII

Passages incorporated into Constantine VII Porphyrogennetos' *De administrando imperio* and *De thematibus* preserve fuller material from the *Ethnika*. These extracts show that a rich form of the work remained accessible in the tenth century. They also warn against treating the surviving epitome's attribution and wording as the complete Stephanean state.

### 5. Eustathios and the twelfth-century lexica

Eustathios of Thessalonike cites Stephanos extensively and sometimes preserves material absent from the surviving epitome. Knauss, Diller, and Billerbeck have used these citations to infer that Eustathios had a **richer recension or epitome**. The *Etymologicum Symeonis*, *Etymologicum Magnum*, and related lexica also preserve or rework Stephanean material. Their exact genealogical relationships are difficult because lexica regularly copy, combine, and reattribute material.

This indirect tradition is the strongest argument that the surviving text is not simply a transparent copy of one sixth-century act of abbreviation.

### 6. The uneven surviving text

Alpha is predictably fuller, as often happens at the beginning of lexica. Pi, Rho, and Sigma are unusually terse; Chi through Omega become fuller again and include more citations. The beginning of Chi is transmitted twice in one manuscript, once in a reduced form and once in a fuller form. This pattern is compatible with recensional mixture or successive reduction.

It is not sufficient by itself to identify an epitomator. Letter size, source distribution, subject matter, exemplar quality, and ordinary scribal loss can all affect density.

### 7. Physical loss and ordinary textual corruption

Major gaps in Kappa and Lambda, at the end of Omicron, and at the beginning of Pi are attributable to missing leaves already absent from the lost archetype shared by the main manuscripts. They are **not deliberate epitomisation**. The late manuscript tradition also contains corruptions, interpolations, and humanist conjectures. Any theory that treats all absence or incoherence as abbreviation overstates the evidence.

### 8. The Renaissance manuscript family

The direct manuscript tradition of the surviving epitome begins only in the second half of the fifteenth century in northern Italy. The common archetype is lost. The rapid copying of the text and the Aldine edition of 1502 show intense humanist interest, but they do not independently reveal where or when the last medieval reductions occurred.

## II. The current theories of epitomisation

The theories are best understood as a family of models rather than mutually exclusive propositions. For example, a historical Hermolaos may have made a sixth-century epitome **and** later anonymous readers may have reduced it further.

### A. Who was Hermolaos?

No independent biography, manuscript subscription, dedication, or securely attributed passage survives. Current possibilities are:

1. **A contemporary Constantinopolitan grammarian** who genuinely abridged Stephanos for Justinian I.
2. **A later grammarian under Justinian II**, if the *Suda*'s Justinian belongs to the late seventh century.
3. **Stephanos' successor or editor**, who brought the work to completion or created an initial accessible edition, rather than the radically shortened text now extant.
4. **A convenient name attached by the tradition to a process involving several people**. On this view, “Hermolaos' epitome” is genealogically real but the surviving wording is mainly the work of anonymous later reducers.
5. **A notice affected by catalogue transmission**: historical at its core but with dedication or chronology displaced between Stephanos and the epitomator.

There is no strong basis for identifying him with any other known Hermolaos. Any biographical detail beyond “Constantinopolitan grammarian” should be labelled conjectural.

### B. When was the work abbreviated?

The broad chronological constraints are firmer than the individual stages:

- **c. 528–573, probably c. 530–550**: composition and redaction of the original.
- **sixth century**: possible Hermolaos edition or epitome if the *Suda* is read literally with Justinian I.
- **tenth century**: Constantine VII has access to a full or substantially fuller text.
- **eleventh century**: the fuller Delta fragment is copied into Coislinianus 228.
- **by the twelfth century**: Eustathios and etymological lexica use an abridged but often richer state.
- **before the later fifteenth century**: the lost archetype of the direct epitome manuscripts has acquired its current lacunae and mixed density.

The surviving evidence therefore rules out the idea that the full work vanished immediately after composition. It does **not** rule out an early epitome circulating alongside the full work.

### C. How was it abbreviated?

The directly demonstrated procedure is mainly **subtractive**:

1. remove all or most of a literary quotation;
2. sometimes retain the author's name, work, or book number, but often remove these too;
3. collapse a chain of witnesses to one or none;
4. remove analogical parallels used to justify a derivation;
5. remove secondary ethnic forms, feminine forms, alternative spellings, and rival etymologies;
6. remove historical, mythographic, paradoxographic, and cultural explanations;
7. keep the entry's identifying skeleton and the preferred derived form;
8. occasionally join remnants in a way that damages logic or syntax;
9. occasionally leave source wording embedded without attribution.

The current evidence does not support a claim that epitomators systematically rewrote the alphabetical architecture. Contamination and stylistic alteration occur, but the fixed article order constrained large-scale reorganisation.

### D. Why was it abbreviated?

No preface by an epitomator survives, so motives are inferred. In descending order of probability:

1. **Material and economic compression**: a fifty- to sixty-book work was expensive to copy and difficult to house or circulate.
2. **Reference utility**: grammarians primarily needed the headword, category, localization, and derived ethnic; repeated quotations looked expendable once the form had been established.
3. **Pedagogy**: a compact lexical manual suited teaching and consultation better than a cultural encyclopaedia.
4. **Preservation through transformation**: abbreviation made copying more likely and may have helped the work survive changes in libraries and institutions.
5. **Changing intellectual priorities**: later users valued derivational and orthographic answers differently from the antiquarian documentation that supported them.
6. **Imperial presentation or patronage**: possible for an initial edition if the dedication really belonged to Hermolaos, but not enough to explain subsequent reductions.
7. **Specific crisis response**—for example, saving a library copy before or after 1204—is possible within Bouiron's reconstruction but cannot be established from the text.

The paradox is important: epitomisation destroyed much of the evidence modern scholars most want, but it may also be the reason any continuous version survived.

## III. What is presently known: twenty-five leading themes and debates

### 1. Stephanos' identity, career, and date

**State of knowledge:** Almost nothing biographical is secure beyond his association with Constantinople and his status as a *grammatikos*. The internal Justinianic markers strongly favour the sixth century, and his reference to a predecessor at the imperial school makes a teaching post plausible.

**Debate:** Whether he held one of the formal chairs of Greek grammar is an inference from institutional context, not an appointment record. Individual first-person statements may be Stephanean, epitomatorial, or interpolated. The exact composition date depends on whether the reference to Petros Patrikios and other contemporary notices are original.

**Current position:** “Constantinopolitan grammarian active under Justinian I, probably in the second quarter or middle of the sixth century” is responsible; a precise floruit or religious biography is not.

### 2. The imperial school, patronage, and the dedication to Justinian

**State of knowledge:** Stephanos' work fits the classical grammatical curriculum and the scholarly environment of Constantinople. The *Suda* associates a presentation to Justinian with the epitome notice.

**Debate:** Did Stephanos dedicate the original to Justinian I, did Hermolaos dedicate an epitome to Justinian I or II, or did catalogue transmission shift the dedication? Billerbeck and Neumann-Hartmann consider an original dedication more likely. Bouiron treats Hermolaos as a possible successor/editor producing an accessible imperial edition.

**Current position:** Imperial patronage is probable at the level of the original work, but the dedication cannot securely date the surviving epitome.

### 3. Original title, book count, and physical scale

**State of knowledge:** The fuller title in Coislinianus 228 is much broader than the Renaissance *Peri poleōn* (“On Cities”). Marginal book divisions show a very long multivolume work.

**Debate:** Fifty to fifty-five books (Honigmann; recent Fribourg synthesis) versus sixty (Bouiron and some reference works); how evenly the alphabet was distributed; and whether calculated surviving percentages can recover total physical size.

**Current position:** The work was enormous by any reconstruction. Fine-grained page and folio estimates are sensitive to layout, script, article density, and the representativeness of the Delta sample.

### 4. Genre: geography, encyclopaedia, or grammar?

**State of knowledge:** The work arranges geographical and ethnographic names alphabetically but uses them chiefly to establish forms, derivations, spelling, accent, gender, and usage. Stephanos is a grammarian using geographical-cultural material.

**Debate:** Historians frequently mine the text as a geographical encyclopaedia; Bouiron stresses that this can invert its purpose. The modern Fribourg synthesis calls it a geographical-cultural-historical lexicon while retaining grammar as its organizing centre.

**Current position:** It is best understood as **grammar-driven cultural lexicography**. Neither “mere word list” nor “systematic geography” is adequate.

### 5. The architecture of an original entry

**State of knowledge:** Fuller entries commonly include headword; category and location; homonyms and renamed forms; etymologies or eponyms; derived ethnic, local, possessive, feminine, and adverbial forms; rules and parallels; literary attestations; cultural or historical notes.

**Debate:** How fixed this sequence was across the full work, how much Stephanos inherited from source lexica, and whether long entries represent authorial composition or accumulated dossier material.

**Current position:** The structure is regular enough to expose many epitomatorial omissions, but not rigid enough to reconstruct missing content mechanically.

### 6. The lost *protechnologemata*

**State of knowledge:** Stephanos refers to preliminary technical material on the formation of ethnics, and later grammarians may echo a Stephanean *technologia*.

**Debate:** Was this a formal preface, a separate introductory book, a set of preliminary sections, or only internal methodological material? Did epitomisation remove a systematic theory now visible only piecemeal?

**Current position:** Some lost theoretical introduction is plausible, but its extent and content cannot be reconstructed confidently. Fraser emphasizes that the epitome's surviving rules look ad hoc; that may be an effect of loss.

### 7. Hermolaos and the number of epitomisation stages

**State of knowledge:** The *Suda* names Hermolaos; direct comparison proves drastic abbreviation; later indirect witnesses imply richer states.

**Debate:** A single Hermolaos epitome, an early epitome later damaged, successive anonymous reductions, or Bouiron's three-stage medieval process and composite archetype.

**Current position:** Multiple stages are plausible and perhaps likely at a broad level, but the precise stemma and dating remain open. Entry-level analysis should identify symptoms without assigning them automatically to H1, H2, or H3.

### 8. Manuscript stemma, archetype, and physical lacunae

**State of knowledge:** The main direct manuscripts descend from a lost archetype and were copied in fifteenth-century Italy. Shared gaps reveal missing leaves in that archetype. Coislinianus 228 belongs to a different, fuller line.

**Debate:** The upper stemma before the surviving archetype; southern Italy or Otranto as a transmission centre; whether the archetype was deliberately compiled from several recensions; and how humanist corrections entered branches.

**Current position:** The late direct tradition and lost archetype impose a hard ceiling on certainty. Physical loss must be modelled separately from epitomisation.

### 9. Reconstructing the original and the legitimacy of negative evidence

**State of knowledge:** Fuller witnesses and internal cross-references permit local reconstruction of some lost material. Missing quotations and even missing entries are demonstrable.

**Debate:** How much can be inferred from absence? Can a missing place, author, ethnic form, or doctrine be treated as absent from Stephanos, or only from the surviving epitome?

**Current position:** Negative evidence is usually weak unless the relevant witness is known to preserve that class of information consistently. “Not in the epitome” must not become “not in Stephanos.”

### 10. Cross-references as fossils of the fuller work

**State of knowledge:** Neumann-Hartmann identified fifty-five internal cross-references in the epitome. Some point to entries that no longer contain the promised information.

**Debate:** Whether dangling references result from deliberate cuts, lost leaves, altered headwords, or source inheritance; and how much of the original plan they reveal.

**Current position:** Cross-references are exceptionally valuable structural evidence. Their failure is often a redactional signal, but each requires comparison with the manuscript gap pattern and parallel lexica.

### 11. Stephanos' source architecture: direct reading versus intermediaries

**State of knowledge:** More than three hundred authors feed the original work, but a citation does not prove direct consultation. Billerbeck demonstrates that Stephanos read Pausanias directly in many cases, while Artemidoros normally reached him through Marcianus' epitome. Oros, Herodian, scholia, earlier geographic lexica, and author-specific collections mediated much material.

**Debate:** Which authors Stephanos read directly; whether linguistic fingerprints can identify relay sources; and when the epitomator rather than Stephanos removed or confused attribution.

**Current position:** Source relationships must be argued author by author and entry by entry. A flat list of named authorities is not a source history.

### 12. The grammatical inheritance: Epaphroditos, Herodian, and Oros

**State of knowledge:** These grammarians are central to the system of derivation, accentuation, orthography, Homeric scholarship, and ethnic formation behind the *Ethnika*. Oros' work on how ethnics should be expressed has long been regarded as a major source.

**Debate:** Whether Oros was the principal immediate template; how much Herodian entered through Oros or other lexica; and whether Bouiron's “source lexicon” derived from Diogenianus and annotated by Oros can be reconstructed.

**Current position:** Deep dependence on earlier grammatical traditions is established. A single linear source genealogy is not.

### 13. Citation technique and the survival of fragments

**State of knowledge:** The full work justified forms with quotations. Abbreviation often leaves only an author, work, book, paraphrase, or unattributed phrase. The *Ethnika* is therefore a major witness to otherwise lost poets, historians, geographers, grammarians, and antiquarians.

**Debate:** Whether a fragment is verbatim, paraphrased, mediated, clipped by Stephanos, clipped by an epitomator, or silently incorporated. Modern fragment editions sometimes overstate the directness of the witness.

**Current position:** Every “fragment” needs a transmission statement. Attribution, wording, and directness are separate claims.

### 14. Ethnic formation, analogy, and local usage

**State of knowledge:** The central technical problem is deriving demonyms and related adjectives from place and people names. Stephanos uses analogical rules but also records exceptions, local forms, rival forms, and normative judgements such as “it ought to be.”

**Debate:** How systematic the lost theory was; whether anomalies are authentic usage, analogical repair, or epitomatorial damage; and how political communities differ from natural features or other names that cannot strictly generate an ethnic.

**Current position:** Morphological analysis must attend simultaneously to rule, attested usage, dialect, and the object denoted. Fraser's *Greek Ethnic Terminology* remains the principal large-scale linguistic framework.

### 15. Orthography, accentuation, dialect, gender, and inflection

**State of knowledge:** Orthographic and prosodic correctness belonged to the grammarian's educational role. Entries preserve rare declensions, feminine formations, variant vowels, dialect forms, and accentual judgements.

**Debate:** Whether an irregular form belongs to the cited author, local usage, Stephanos' norm, an epitomator's simplification, or manuscript corruption. Accents in late manuscripts can be especially unstable.

**Current position:** A form should not be normalized merely because it violates the expected rule. The authority and transmission layer of each form matter.

### 16. Etymology, eponymy, and paretymology

**State of knowledge:** Stephanos preserves multiple kinds of name explanation: linguistic derivation, founding eponym, mythic ancestor, physical characteristic, and learned or popular wordplay. He often transmits competing explanations rather than selecting one.

**Debate:** The historical value of eponymous stories; whether Stephanos evaluates or merely compiles; and how much mythographic material has been shortened or detached from its source. Fowler has shown that anonymous wording may preserve overlooked early mythography, but only with careful formulaic comparison.

**Current position:** Etymologies are evidence for ancient scholarship and cultural memory first, and for modern historical linguistics only after independent testing.

### 17. Metonomasia, polyonymy, Homeric names, and endonyms

**State of knowledge:** The work records formal renamings, alternative poetic or mythic names, multiple names, older Homeric forms, and locally used endonyms. Neumann-Hartmann has differentiated these categories.

**Debate:** Whether a “renaming” describes a historical administrative act, parallel literary usage, chronological replacement, or confusion between homonymous places.

**Current position:** Alternative-name relations should be typed rather than collapsed into a single alias field. The date and authority for each name are essential.

### 18. Site classification and historical-geographical reliability

**State of knowledge:** Entries classify poleis, villages, demes, islands, rivers, mountains, regions, and peoples, often with relative localization. Whitehead and later commentators show both substantial value and recurrent imprecision.

**Debate:** Whether a label reflects the status in Stephanos' own time, in his source's time, or a lexicographical category; how to identify sites that moved or shared names; and whether apparent geographical error originates with a source, Stephanos, an epitomator, or a scribe.

**Current position:** The *Ethnika* is a powerful but layered witness. Archaeology, inscriptions, coins, and other literary sources are necessary controls.

### 19. Chronological layering and the “timeless” map

**State of knowledge:** Stephanos places information from archaic poetry, Classical historiography, Hellenistic scholarship, Roman geographers, and late antique administrative reality side by side.

**Debate:** Older scholarship sometimes treated this as poor historical method. More recent work sees it as normal lexicographical compilation oriented to name forms, not a synchronized historical atlas.

**Current position:** Every geographical or political statement needs a source-date as well as a Stephanos-date. Temporal mismatch is often a property of the compilation, not simply an error.

### 20. Peoples, ethnography, and identities

**State of knowledge:** The *Ethnika* preserves names and descriptions of peoples across the inhabited world, including groups poorly attested elsewhere. It records Greek classificatory language and long chains of inherited ethnography.

**Debate:** Whether an ethnonym denotes a self-identified group, an external label, a political unit, a literary archaism, or a grammatical derivative; and how “ethnic” terminology maps onto modern concepts of ethnicity.

**Current position:** Names of peoples should not be reified automatically. The semantic, political, chronological, and source contexts must be separated.

### 21. Foundations, migrations, colonisation, and civic origins

**State of knowledge:** Foundation legends and eponyms connect cities to heroes, colonists, migrations, rulers, and mother-cities. These notices are heavily used in polis and colonial history.

**Debate:** Whether a notice preserves early local memory, Hellenistic civic myth-making, antiquarian systematisation, or late compilation; and whether contradictions reflect real competing traditions or damaged source chains.

**Current position:** Foundation accounts are valuable evidence for traditions **about** origins. They become evidence for an historical foundation only with independent corroboration.

### 22. Mythography and religion

**State of knowledge:** The *Ethnika* preserves divine epithets, sanctuaries, oracles, cult aetiologies, heroic genealogies, and otherwise lost mythographic variants. Place-name explanation is often the mechanism that brings this material into the lexicon.

**Debate:** Source identification, the date of anonymous myths, and whether an epitomated grammatical context has obscured the original narrative logic.

**Current position:** The work is an important fragmentary mythographic witness, but formulaic resemblance alone does not establish an early author or direct quotation.

### 23. Historians, poets, and lost literature

**State of knowledge:** Stephanos is often the sole or principal witness for lost local historians, ethnographical poems, Hellenistic poets, and minor geographers. Studies of Rhianos, Euphorion, Parthenios, Demosthenes of Bithynia, Lycophron, Homeric scholarship, Hecataeus, and others show the range.

**Debate:** Direct reading versus excerpt collections; how much quotation was removed; whether a named author supports the whole surrounding statement or only a form; and the circularity of reconstructing a lost author from an epitome and then using that reconstruction to emend the epitome.

**Current position:** The *Ethnika* is indispensable for fragment studies, but the fragment's boundary and relay history must be explicit.

### 24. Famous citizens, mirabilia, proverbs, and cultural encyclopaedism

**State of knowledge:** The fuller work included lists of notable citizens, wonders, paradoxographical material, proverbs, natural phenomena, and local cultural lore. The 2021 synthesis shows that these are integral to the original lexicographical project, not random ornament.

**Debate:** Whether these dossiers came directly from specialist collections such as Herennios Philon's civic work and paradoxographical compilations; what function they served; and how representative the thin epitome is.

**Current position:** Their survival is uneven because exactly this material was vulnerable to abbreviation. Absence of a cultural dossier in a short entry means little.

### 25. Christianity, Byzantine reception, and humanist rediscovery

**State of knowledge:** Stephanos worked in Justinianic Christian Constantinople while transmitting overwhelmingly classical material. Some explicitly Christian phrases in the epitome interrupt normal entry form and are likely later insertions. Constantine VII, the *Suda*, etymological lexica, Tzetzes, and Eustathios reused the work; Ermolao Barbaro, Poliziano, Erasmus, and Aldus transformed its Renaissance reception.

**Debate:** Stephanos' personal religion; the date and origin of Christianizing glosses; which Byzantine readers used the full work or which epitome; and how humanist conjecture shaped the direct text.

**Current position:** Institutional Christianity is certain; a detailed confessional portrait of Stephanos is not. Reception witnesses are both evidence for lost text and independent acts of adaptation.

## IV. A compact state-of-knowledge map

### Established or close to established

- The work belongs to the Justinianic sixth century and was composed in Constantinople.
- Its organizing purpose is grammatical and lexicographical, though its content is geographically and culturally encyclopaedic.
- It was originally enormous and contained far more quotations, authorities, grammatical parallels, and cultural material than the surviving epitome.
- Selective excision of quotations and supporting material is directly demonstrated.
- A full or much fuller text survived into the tenth and eleventh centuries.
- The direct surviving manuscript tradition derives from a lost, already defective late archetype.
- Some gaps result from missing leaves, not epitomisation.
- Named-source statements may be direct, mediated, paraphrased, clipped, or misframed; those are separate textual questions.

### Probable

- Stephanos taught grammar in the orbit of the imperial school.
- The original work, rather than Hermolaos' epitome alone, was dedicated to Justinian I.
- Eustathios used a richer form than the direct epitome manuscripts preserve.
- More than one state of abbreviation circulated.
- Material economy and reference utility were principal motives for abbreviation.

### Open

- Whether Hermolaos was contemporary with Stephanos or later.
- Whether “Justinian” in the epitome notice is Justinian I or II.
- Whether Coislinianus 228 preserves the original or an early fuller recension.
- Fifty to fifty-five versus sixty books.
- One principal epitome plus later damage versus several deliberate reductions.
- The precise relationships among Eustathios, the etymological lexica, and the direct epitome.
- The date and extent of the lost theoretical introduction.

### Speculative

- Exact reduction percentages for the entire work extrapolated from a small sample.
- Assigning particular letter blocks to H1, H2, or H3 without independent controls.
- Dating individual reductions tightly to the early twelfth, post-1204, or fourteenth century.
- Locating the construction of the common archetype specifically at Otranto or San Nicola di Casole.
- Explaining unusually full author clusters by a particular medieval scholar's tastes without manuscript evidence.
- Recovering an epitomator's personal intention from the fact of omission alone.

## V. Implications for the seven specialist skills

The central change should be conceptual: each specialist should ask **which textual layer a claim concerns**. A finding about “Stephanos” is not equivalent to a finding about the fuller recension, the epitome, the common archetype, one manuscript, or a modern editorial reconstruction.

### Shared requirements for all specialists

Each finding should, where relevant, identify:

- `claim_target_layer`: `stephanos_original`, `fuller_recension`, `early_epitome`, `surviving_epitome`, `lost_archetype`, `individual_manuscript`, or `modern_editor`;
- `witness_type`: direct epitome, fuller Delta witness, Constantinian excerpt, Eustathian quotation, other lexicon, apparatus, or external parallel;
- `redaction_signal`: deleted quotation, dangling cross-reference, collapsed source chain, missing derivational parallel, syntactic seam, unattributed quotation residue, duplicated recension, or none;
- `alternative_mechanism`: physical lacuna, scribal error, contamination, interpolation, source error, or unresolved;
- `source_relation`: direct, mediated, parallel, excerpted, uncertain;
- `negative_evidence_strength`: invalid, weak, moderate, or strong;
- a bounded confidence statement and the strongest counter-explanation.

These are recommendations for specialist reasoning and finding design, not a proposal to bypass the relational workflow or write database tables directly.

### 1. `$textual-critic`

**Strengthen the focus on:**

- distinguishing deliberate reduction from physical loss and ordinary corruption;
- locating syntax that has been damaged by the removal of a quotation or connective tissue;
- comparing direct fuller witnesses and indirect quotations before proposing emendation;
- duplicated or mixed recensions, especially where one manuscript preserves fuller and shorter forms;
- whether a conjecture silently reconstructs the lost original or only repairs the surviving epitome.

**Avoid:** calling every short, ungrammatical, or abrupt passage an epitomatorial error. Apparatus variants remain the primary entry-level evidence.

### 2. `$lexicographer`

**Strengthen the focus on:**

- the difference between a stated rule, an attested literary form, local usage, and Stephanos' normative preference;
- lost analogical parallels signalled by a bare conclusion or unexplained *hōs* comparison;
- families of ethnic, feminine, possessive, local, and adverbial derivatives rather than the headline demonym alone;
- dialect, accent, quantity, gender, and inflection as potentially source-layered;
- whether an object can properly generate an *ethnikon* or is included by analogical extension.

**Avoid:** regularising an exceptional form merely because the supporting quotation has been cut.

### 3. `$source-critic`

**Strengthen the focus on:**

- a source relay graph rather than a flat cited-author list;
- the boundary of what the named author actually supports;
- quotation, paraphrase, indirect excerpt, and unattributed embedded source wording as distinct states;
- the possibility that Stephanos used an epitome of the cited work, as with Artemidoros through Marcianus;
- citations lost from a chain and rival authorities reduced to one survivor;
- circular reconstructions in fragment editions.

**Avoid:** “Stephanos cites X, therefore Stephanos read X directly.”

### 4. `$historical-geographer`

**Strengthen the focus on:**

- source-date, referent-date, and Stephanos-date as separate temporal fields;
- site category versus historical constitutional status;
- homonymy, metonomasia, polyonymy, endonym, exonym, and chronological renaming as different relations;
- ancient localization language rather than modern coordinate matching alone;
- the possibility that a short localization has lost its qualifying quotation or rival identification;
- archaeology, epigraphy, and numismatics as controls, with disagreements preserved.

**Avoid:** treating the alphabetic lexicon as a synchronized sixth-century map.

### 5. `$stephanos-specialist`

This role should become the principal **entry-architecture and redaction specialist**, not a generalist who duplicates the other five.

**Core questions:**

1. Which expected parts of a Stephanean article survive: identification, localization, homonyms, metonomasia, etymology, derivations, authorities, rule parallels, cultural dossier?
2. Does the entry contain a recognizable redactional seam: dangling particle, unsupported judgement, orphaned cross-reference, anonymous quotation residue, abrupt source switch, or duplicated formulation?
3. Which voice is speaking: Stephanos, an earlier grammarian, a quoted author, an epitomator, a glossator, or a modern supplement?
4. Does the article preserve a formula recurring elsewhere in a way that reconstructs function without reconstructing lost words?
5. Is an apparent anomaly better explained by source inheritance, Stephanos' compilation, epitomisation, or manuscript transmission?

**Avoid:** turning “epitomisation” into a free-standing claim without a local symptom and an alternative explanation.

### 6. `$translation-critic`

**Strengthen the focus on:**

- telegraphic syntax as a feature of the surviving epitome;
- not silently supplying relations destroyed by abbreviation;
- preserving uncertainty about the scope of a source name or quotation;
- distinguishing Stephanos' assertion from a cited authority's assertion and from the epitomator's join;
- technical consistency for ethnic, local, possessive, feminine, metonomasia, homonymy, and grammatical judgement;
- additions needed for readable English being marked as supplied, not presented as recovered original content.

**Avoid:** smoothing an incoherent epitomated sentence into a confident historical proposition.

### 7. `$scholarly-verifier`

**Strengthen the focus on:**

- verifying both the proposition and the claimed textual layer;
- rejecting a finding that infers original absence from epitome absence without a valid negative-evidence argument;
- requiring one verdict per atomic proposition, especially when a finding combines form, source, date, and location;
- checking whether the evidence supports direct use, only parallel wording, or an indirect relay;
- grouping revision requests by a bounded defect type rather than by specialist identity;
- accepting “unresolved between epitomisation and corruption” when the evidence does not discriminate.

**Release criterion:** no finding should be release-ready if it presents a recensional hypothesis as a fact about Stephanos' original.

## VI. Recommended research agenda for this project

1. **Build a witness-layer ontology.** Make every specialist finding target a textual or reception layer explicitly.
2. **Create a controlled redaction-symptom vocabulary.** This allows corpus-wide comparison without pretending to know the epitomator's identity.
3. **Index the fifty-five internal cross-references.** Mark fulfilled, partially fulfilled, dangling, or lost-target references in the current relational corpus.
4. **Use the fuller Delta articles as a calibration set.** Test what each specialist would have inferred from the epitome alone, then measure the inference against S. This is more informative than measuring length alone.
5. **Build source-relay exemplars.** Pausanias as a likely direct source and Artemidoros-through-Marcianus as a mediated source should become contrasting training cases.
6. **Separate deliberate reduction from leaf loss.** Store known archetypal lacuna zones so specialists do not diagnose them as editorial preference.
7. **Measure entry composition, not just entry length.** Counts of authorities, quotations, derivational forms, connectives, cross-references, and formulae are more diagnostic than characters retained.
8. **Control corpus comparisons.** Any letter-level “epitomiser fingerprint” must control for source, topic, headword class, and manuscript lacunae. Volume or letter is only a proxy.
9. **Treat negative evidence formally.** A finding should state why a missing item was expected to survive this type and layer of entry.
10. **Keep scholarship and permitted evidence separate.** Modern research should shape rubrics, questions, and verification logic; the entry-level finding must still be supported by the copyright-safe dossier and its typed evidence.

## Final assessment

The *Ethnika* is now much better edited and conceptually understood than it was before the five-volume Fribourg edition. The principal advance is a shift away from treating it as a defective geographical encyclopaedia and toward reading it as a **layered grammatical lexicon whose cultural riches have been selectively thinned by transmission**. The largest unresolved problem is no longer whether it was abbreviated—it unquestionably was—but how many distinct recensional acts intervene between Stephanos and the fifteenth-century archetype, and which surviving irregularities belong to which act.

For this project, the productive research question is therefore not “Did Hermolaos do this?” but:

> What local evidence shows that this entry has lost, combined, or displaced a particular kind of material, and which transmission mechanism best explains it?

That question is answerable within a bounded dossier. The personal history of the epitomators usually is not.
